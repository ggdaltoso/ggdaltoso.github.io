Back around 2017 I bought a Raspberry Pi Zero after watching a pile of videos
about the fun things you could do with one. One of them stuck with me and kept
me thinking about it for a long time: retrogaming.

The more I read about it, the more I wanted in. It wasn't only the old games I
wanted to play again. It was the idea of building a device and having something
100% mine.

That's how I found channels like [Wermy/Sudomod](https://www.youtube.com/@wermy)
and [GreatScott](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=_MtN_s4tVK0). I still go back
to both.

Building things I don't know how to build has always pushed me to research and
study topics I never imagined I'd be able to work with.

Most of what I know about software came from my career in development, but
hardware always pulled at me too. So I decided to build my own console.

The software side I had already solved, and I even wrote here about [setting up
an emulation station with a Raspberry
Pi](/en/build-a-videogame-with-raspberry-pi). What I was missing was the device
itself: the screen, the battery, the buttons, the shell.

![The bench mid-build: the outline scored into the PVC, the shell already drilled, the Pi board and the little screen](/media/alexo/videogame-diy.jpg 'The build | 320')

It didn't go well.

The idea of having something of my own stayed with me, and every video I watched
and every post I read brought it back. Time passed and the project sat there.
The parts just collecting dust.

One day I decided to build something simple, something that only needed the Pi
and the screen. That's when it hit me to put what I know about development to
work.

And that's how Alexo was born.

A device that sits next to my PC showing some photos, the time, the forecast and
the dollar and euro rates. Dressed as
[React95](/en/react95-fun-side-project-of-the-year), of course.

![Two Alexo screens: the gallery with the clock on top, the forecast with the player running below](/media/alexo/alexo-telas.png 'Alexo | 320')

### What runs in there

Alexo is a web app, and the setup is less dignified than that sounds. The Pi
runs a Node server and, on the same machine, opens a fullscreen Chromium
pointed back at itself. What you see on that 3.5 inch screen is a browser
sitting on `localhost`.

The interface is React. The server behind it serves that page, holds the photos
and the music, and talks to the NFC reader.

### The player

I had a handful of NFC tags I'd bought once meaning to automate the house: at
bedtime, when I put my phone down on the nightstand, it would read one of them
and turn off all the lights. Never built it.

Then I saw a [video from Nvkv
Makes](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=hf311daZqMk), who built a screen-free
music player for his kids with an ESP32 and NFC cards shaped like cassette
tapes, each one pointing at a folder of music. That was the push my tags needed
to leave the drawer. His is a dedicated device, built from scratch, with no
screen at all. Mine is almost the opposite: a screen that was already on my desk
and got a reader bolted on.

Touch a tag, an album plays. Take it off, it pauses. Put the same one back, it
picks up where it stopped. Touch a different one, the album changes.

Each tag is a whole album, never a single track. And among the albums I put a
few old game soundtracks, which suit the Windows 95 look of the thing and pull
on the same nostalgia.

The music isn't played by my code. It's [mpv](https://mpv.io/), a command line
player that sits running as a background service. Alexo's backend just sends it
orders, like "play this album" or "pause".

The player has no controls on screen, and it wouldn't make sense for it to have
any. The screen isn't a touchscreen and the device doesn't have a single button,
so it's 100% read-only. It only shows. The tag is what gives orders.

### Where I set it all up

If the screen doesn't take touch and the device has no buttons, how do I add a
new album or change the photos in the gallery?

From another machine. Alexo serves an admin page on the home network, and I open
it from my PC or my phone.

That's where I upload photos and add albums. And that's where my favourite part
lives. To register a tag, I touch it to the reader and the page shows its code
right away. I pick an album from a list, click map, and that tag now plays that
record.

![The admin screen where tags are paired with albums](/media/alexo/admin-music.png 'Registering tags | 320')

There's a machine side too: memory, disk, temperature, and Alexo's services with
buttons to stop and start them.

### The screen wasn't made for this

Alexo's screen is the same one I'd bought years earlier for the console. A cheap
car reversing camera display, which is what a lot of people use for this kind of
project. It takes composite AV rather than HDMI, which on its own means a series
of adjustments on the Pi before anything shows up.

The other problem is power. Cars run on 12V and the Pi gives you 5V. I had done
that conversion once before, years ago, but this screen was a newer model and
none of the tutorials I found applied, because the driver boards were all
different from mine.

I asked for help on
[r/eletronica](https://www.reddit.com/r/eletronica/comments/1vj9q9k/ajuda_para_identificarmodificar_tela_de_r%C3%A9_de/).
The first answer was the obvious one, a 5V to 12V step-up board, which would
have worked. But a much better observation came with it. Plenty of cheap car
displays sold as 12V already have a regulator right at the input and run on 5V
inside. The move was to measure the voltage at a particular capacitor on the
board and, if it read 5V, feed it there directly.

That was exactly the case. No converter needed, just skip the 12V input and hand
the 5V to the point where the screen was already working.

### The four times I was the one who was wrong

#### The reader that was fine all along

The NFC reader wouldn't respond. I'd touch a tag to it and the Pi gave no sign
of having noticed anything at all.

Two explanations seemed obvious, and I argued for both with conviction:

1. The reader wasn't getting enough power
2. A part was missing in between to protect the Pi

Both were wrong. The power was always enough and the part was never needed.

The problem wasn't electrical, it was a conversation. The Pi and the reader can
talk over two different paths, like two languages, and the one I had picked is
the only one of the two where there's no way to ask "anyone there?". You talk
and hope.

All I had to do was switch languages. Except the reader was soldered straight
onto the pins of the first one, no jumpers, and switching meant desoldering
everything and rewiring it somewhere else on the board.

That's when I found out Linux does that switch in software, with a driver called
[`i2c-gpio`](https://github.com/raspberrypi/firmware/blob/master/boot/overlays/README).
The entire fix is one line in the Pi's config file:

```
dtoverlay=i2c-gpio,i2c_gpio_sda=15,i2c_gpio_scl=14,bus=3
```

It builds a new bus on the very same two pins the reader was already soldered
to. Not a single wire moved.

It worked on the first try. And
[`i2cdetect`](https://manpages.debian.org/stable/i2c-tools/i2cdetect.8.en.html),
a one line command, would have told me on day one which of the two languages the
reader was willing to speak.

#### The meter that measured wrong

With the reader working, the readings looked unstable. I'd leave a tag sitting
on it and it would read once, then never again.

To measure it properly I wrote a separate script that read in a loop and counted
how many attempts landed. I tried three different ways of hunting for the tag
and they all came out at the same 2% hit rate. I spent a good while with a
finger holding the tag in place, watching that number refuse to climb.

The problem was the script doing the measuring, not the reader. It asked for
more bytes than the response held, and the difference came out of the next
response. From there on every command read the leftovers of the one before it
and everything came back scrambled. The hardware was perfect the whole time.

The worst part is that the answer had been in front of me from the start.
Another script of mine, this one with the right sizes, read tags without failing
on the same afternoon my meter was insisting on 2%. I suspected the signal, the
antenna position and the protocol before I suspected the tool I had written to
measure.

With the size fixed, it read 100% of the time, on the first attempt.

#### The dog photo

This one is my favourite.

The Pi started dropping off the network every few hours. The symptom was odd.
The router showed the device connected, and nothing answered. No SSH, no ARP.
And the screen kept working normally, not least because everything it shows
comes from `localhost`.

I tested and ruled out three causes:

1. Power. I had just added an audio amplifier and suspected the way I was
   powering the Pi couldn't keep up (it was either a plain USB port or a power
   bank). Luckily the Pi records every time the power dips below the minimum on
   its own, and the record was empty.
2. The remote access tunnel I had set up so I could develop on my own machine
   instead of being stuck working on the Pi. I removed the tunnel and the drops
   carried on.
3. The NFC reader, which scans for tags non-stop. This one had a real, measured
   effect: with scanning on, Wi-Fi fell from 43 to 6.5 Mbps and pings reached
   433ms or failed outright. With only that turned off, 52 Mbps and not a single
   failure in five minutes. But I cut the scanning by a factor of ten and the Pi
   kept dropping.

The real cause was the gallery photos. Phone photos at original resolution, the
largest one 3468×4624. The file is small, 344 KB, because JPEG compresses well.
But to draw it on screen the browser has to decompress it, and then every pixel
becomes 4 bytes.

```
3468 × 4624 × 4 bytes  =  61 MB of RAM   the largest photo alone
the 5 gallery photos   = 190 MB
total RAM on the device= 430 MB
the gallery panel      = 223 × 271 pixels
```

I was decoding 16 million pixels to show 60 thousand.

![The gallery panel measured on Alexo's screen: 223 pixels wide by 271 tall](/media/alexo/gallery-size.png "The panel's real size | 320")

The browser ate so much memory processing those photos that the RAM ran out.
When that happens, the system starts parking on the SD card whatever no longer
fits, and fetching it back whenever it needs it.

And that's how I found out first-hand what swap is 🫠.

The trouble is that this back and forth never stops. The browser keeps asking
for that memory all the time, so the card stays busy all the time. And on the Pi
Zero the card and the Wi-Fi share the same path.

With the card permanently busy, the Wi-Fi never got a turn and dropped its
connection to the router.

A photo of my dog was knocking the Pi off the network.

After resizing the photos down to 800 pixels at most, the 190 MB of decoded
image became 8.2 MB, free memory went from 80 MB to around 250 MB, and the drops
stopped.

#### 10 °C from a paused player

Two days after everything was working, the Pi got extremely hot, kind of out of
nowhere.

My first theory was the surroundings. It had moved off a power bank and onto the
PC's USB, and the stack of boards (Pi, amplifier, screen driver) all sits
touching. I ruled that out the same afternoon. The device settled at 60 °C in
the same spot, on the same cable, without me changing a thing.

There were two causes, and both were software.

The first was mpv writing thirteen log lines per second. Running in a terminal,
this is what it shows:

```
$ mpv "04 Northern Kremisphere.mp3"

Playing: 04 Northern Kremisphere.mp3
 (+) Audio --aid=1 (mp3 2ch 44100Hz)
File tags:
 Artist: Eveline Fischer
 Album: Donkey Kong Country 3
 Title: Northern Kremisphere
A: 00:00:03 / 00:02:46 (1%)
```

In a terminal it all looks lovely. The elapsed line updates in place, because
mpv sends a `\r` at the end of each update and the terminal puts the cursor back
at the start of the line. Which doesn't work so well when you're running the
player in the background, as a systemd service. There's no terminal there at
all, so the output goes to the _journal_, which is where systemd keeps service
logs. And the _journal_ never rewrites anything, it only appends. Every update
became a new line.

Thirteen minutes of music produced 10,096 lines, 78% of the entire _journal_ for
that day. That's constant writing to the SD card, the same path as the dog photo
story. A `--quiet` on the line that starts mpv sorted it out.

The second was that pausing doesn't release the sound card.

The temperature kept climbing _after_ the music stopped.

mpv holds on to the card even while paused. And as long as it's open, the Pi
keeps sending signal to the amplifier non-stop, even when what's going through
is only silence. The amplifier sees that signal and doesn't shut down. It stays
awake, heating up for nothing. Only a stop lets go of everything.

Leaving the player paused keeps the Pi 10 °C hotter.

There was a hardware fix available, poking a pin on the amplifier that puts it
to sleep. But everything was already soldered and I didn't want to touch the
boards again, so I went the software route expecting a bodge.

The mpv version running on Alexo is old and has no option at all for releasing
the sound card. What it does let you do is change the audio output with the
player already running.

So what I do is switch the output to nothing. mpv lets go of the card and still
keeps the queue, the track and the exact second it was on. When it's time to
come back, I hand the real output back and it carries on from where it was, as
if nothing had happened.

A 60 second idle timer does that on its own, and the problem disappears without
anyone knowing it was ever there. No bodge in the end. It came out better than
poking that pin would have.

### The pattern

The mistake was always closer than I thought. I only noticed it rereading my own
notes from that week.

It was the wrong language and not a lack of power, my meter and not the reader,
the photo in the gallery and not the power supply, the paused player and not the
heat from the PC. Power took the blame twice, in two problems that had nothing
to do with each other.

The hypothesis was always more interesting than the cause. And the cause was
always something dumb I had built myself two hours earlier.

What I take from this project has nothing to do with NFC. When my measurement
says one thing and another test of mine says another, the one that's wrong is
usually the thing I wrote to measure with. I learned that halfway through and
still fell for it again later.

### There's always one more thing

Alexo is never finished. A new idea for something to put on it keeps turning up,
and having it switched on there on the desk is what makes me come back.

The console I wanted in 2017 never existed. But there's a 3.5 inch screen next
to my PC that starts playing an album because I touched a tag to it. It isn't
what I had planned, and I like it better this way.
